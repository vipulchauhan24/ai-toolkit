# Copy for events, launches and waitlists

These pages have a deadline. The reader needs to know what it is, when, and whether to act now. Everything else follows.

## Events: conferences, meetups, webinars, workshops

### Who reads it

Someone deciding whether this is worth the time and money, sometimes needing to convince a manager.

Their questions, in order:

1. What is it?
2. When and where?
3. Is it for someone like me?
4. Who is speaking, and about what?
5. What does it cost?
6. How do I get a place?

### Fact sheet: ask for these

- Name, date, start and end time, time zone.
- Venue and full address, or the online platform.
- Who it is for, and the level.
- Speakers who have confirmed, with their titles and talk titles.
- Schedule. Ticket types, prices, deadlines, refund terms.
- What past attendees said, past numbers, sponsors, all real.

### Sections

| Section | How to write it |
|---|---|
| Hero | The name, the date and the place in the first screen. If people have to hunt for the date, you have lost them. One button |
| Who it's for | A few specific lines. Helps people decide and justify the cost |
| Speakers | Name, role, talk title. Only confirmed people |
| Schedule | Times with the time zone, session titles, speakers |
| Tickets | Each type: name, price, what's included, deadline, one button |
| Venue and travel | Full address, map link, transit, accessibility |
| Past event proof | Real numbers, real quotes, real sponsors |
| FAQ | Refunds, recordings, dress, code of conduct |

### Writing

- Talk titles and speaker bios come from the speakers. Do not invent or polish them.
- Put time zones on online events: "14:00 UTC".
- Say what someone will be able to do afterwards, not "insights" or "networking opportunities".
- Urgency must be true: real deadlines and real capacity only. Say when early pricing ends.
- After an event ends, update the page: "This event took place on 12 March." Link recordings.

## Launches and waitlists

### Who reads it

Someone curious about something that doesn't exist yet. You're asking for an email address on a promise.

Their questions:

1. What is it?
2. Why should I care?
3. When?
4. What do I get for signing up?
5. What will you do with my email?

### Fact sheet: ask for these

- What it does, as concretely as possible.
- Who it's for, and the problem it solves.
- What exists today: a demo, a screenshot, a prototype.
- Honest timing. What signing up gets: early access, a price, a notification.
- How often you will email.

### Page parts

| Part | How to write it |
|---|---|
| Headline | What it is, plainly. Mystery doesn't earn an email |
| Sub-line | The problem and how this solves it |
| Evidence | A real screen, a demo, or a clear explanation of how it will work |
| Form | One email field, a button that names the result: "Get early access", "Join the waitlist" |
| Beside the form | What they get and how often you'll write: "One email when it's ready." |
| After signing up | Confirm, and set expectations: "You're on the list. We expect to open in June." |

A pre-launch page usually has no proof. Do not invent any: no fake waitlist counts, no made-up quotes. The product's own preview, or a clear account of the problem, does that job.

A launch date is optional. Give one only if you mean it. "Later this year" is better than a date you will miss.

"Coming soon" is allowed on this kind of page, because it is true here. It still can't be the whole message.

## Tone

Events: clear, a little energetic, flat on logistics and refunds. Waitlists: direct, modest about what doesn't exist yet. Excitement comes from how specific you are.

## Checklist

- [ ] Date, time, time zone and place are on the first screen.
- [ ] Each speaker and sponsor is confirmed.
- [ ] All deadlines and capacity claims are true.
- [ ] Waitlist: says what happens after signing up.
- [ ] No invented sign-up counts.
