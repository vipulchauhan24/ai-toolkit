# Copy for a portfolio or personal site

The job of this site is to get the reader to contact you, or to say yes to an interview. It is judged in seconds.

## Who reads it

Hiring managers and recruiters, clients, peers. In NN/g's survey of 204 UX hiring professionals, the message was consistent: they rarely read a portfolio closely, so it has to be scannable and contain no unnecessary detail. They look for the problem you solved, your part in it, and whether it made a difference.

Their questions, in order:

1. What do you do, and at what level?
2. Have you done work like what I need?
3. What exactly did you do, as opposed to your team?
4. Did it work?
5. Can you think clearly and write clearly?
6. How do I reach you?

## Fact sheet: ask for these

- The role you want next, and the work you do not want more of.
- 3 to 5 projects. For each: the problem, your role, team size, constraints, what you tried, what changed, and how it was measured.
- Numbers with a before and after.
- Links to live work and code. Screenshots. A portrait, if you want one.
- What you can't show (confidential work), and what you may say about it.
- Anything that sounds like you: an opinion on your craft, a side project.

## Sections

Use only those you have real material for.

| Section | Job | How to write it |
|---|---|---|
| Hero | What you do and your strongest proof | h1: your name and what you build, or the role. Sub-line: years, specialty, kind of problem. One number beats three adjectives |
| Selected work | 3 to 5 projects, best first | Project name, one line on the problem, one on the result. Link to the case study |
| Case study page | How you think | See below |
| Experience | Where and how long | Company, role, dates, then 2 to 4 bullets that each state a change you caused |
| Skills or stack | Fit check | Group by use. Only what you would be happy to be interviewed on |
| About | That you are a person | A few plain sentences. One thing that isn't work is fine |
| Writing or talks | Depth | Titles and dates, only if real |
| Contact | The action | One way that works. Say what you are open to |

**Leave out** any section with nothing real in it. Two good projects and no "coming soon" cards is better than four empty slots.

Order by what is strongest. An experienced engineer with confidential work may lead with experience. A designer leads with work.

## Headlines

- "Ada Lund builds fast full-stack web apps"
- "Staff engineer for data-heavy products"
- "I design type for small presses"

Avoid "Hi, I'm Ada" as the headline when it says nothing about what you do. A greeting works in the about text.

## Writing a bullet or project line

Each one states **what changed and how you did it**. Start with a verb that names your own action.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Responsible for the frontend of a logistics app | Built the live-tracking map used by delivery riders in three cities |
| Worked on performance improvements | Cut style recalculations from 500 to 100 per second by moving user changes into DuckDB |
| Leveraged cutting-edge technologies to deliver scalable solutions | Moved the API to serverless Node on AWS Lambda and retired two servers |

- Say "I" for what you did and "we" for what the team did. Do not claim the team's work.
- Name the tool only where it matters to the point.
- Cut duties. Keep changes.
- Keep to the two or three bullets that best support the job you want. A list of every task reads as a résumé pasted into a page.

## Case studies

NN/g's list of what hiring managers want, in the order to write it:

1. The problem or the goal, and why it mattered.
2. Your role, and who you worked with.
3. Constraints: time, data, legacy systems.
4. What you tried, including what you rejected and why.
5. What you shipped.
6. What changed, for users and for the business.
7. What you learned.

Keep it scannable: short sections with headings that state the point, and images that carry the story. Show some of the mess. A clean straight line from problem to solution is not believable.

Decide the three things you want a reader to remember, write them down, and check the finished page delivers them.

**Confidential work:** describe the kind of problem and the method, and remove names and numbers you may not share. Say that you have done so.

## Tone

First person. Confident and specific, and never grand. A portfolio is the one place where a little personality in the about text is expected. Let it show in one or two real details.

## Microcopy

- Contact button: what actually happens. "Email me", "Book a call".
- Availability: a plain statement with a date, if true. "Open to senior frontend roles from March." Omit it otherwise.
- Form success: say when you will reply, only if you mean it.

## Checklist

- [ ] First screen says what you do and at what level.
- [ ] Each project states a result, or says honestly why it can't.
- [ ] Every number has a before, a unit or a comparison.
- [ ] The word "passionate" appears nowhere.
- [ ] No placeholder projects.
- [ ] Contact is one click from every page.
